Time Watch

An open design for a watch that holds seconds and pays by touch. It has no buttons: a glass, a fingerprint sensor and two secure chips. Any watch maker may build it.

  • Shows the time, for weeks on one charge.
  • Pays another watch, a phone or a shop when the two touch.
  • Works when the watch itself is offline.
  • Keeps its key in a chip that never gives it out.

What it does

  • A watch first

    The time and the date, always on, readable in sunlight. The same screen shows your seconds.

  • Three gestures

    Turn a finger around the edge to set an amount. Bring two watches together. Rest a finger on the sensor to approve.

  • Seconds and milliseconds

    Plus and minus are the two halves of the glass. One step is a millisecond, a second or a minute.

  • You read before you sign

    After the touch the watch shows who receives, the amount, the fee and the total. Only then does a finger or the PIN send it.

  • A pocket, not a vault

    The watch may spend a set amount a day, 600 seconds unless you change it. Your seconds stay on the network, so a lost watch loses nothing: you remove it from your account.

  • Locks off the wrist

    Finger or PIN once when you put it on. It locks by itself when it leaves your wrist.

  • Offline on your side

    The watch signs with no connection. The other side carries the signed payment to the network.

  • Upgradable

    New firmware arrives through a phone. The watch accepts it only if it is signed by several keys and is newer than what it runs, and it asks you first.

Inside the watch

Nine groups of parts. Open the watch, then choose a number.

  1. Hardened glass over the whole face, with the touch layer under it. There is no button anywhere on the case. The touch layer is wired to the secure processor, never to a radio chip.

  2. A round reflective screen of about 1.2 inches that keeps its picture without power, the kind sports watches use to run for weeks. Black and white, always on, lit by daylight. Only the secure processor can draw on it.

  3. A flat coil around the screen, for the short-range radio that bank cards use (NFC, 13.56 MHz). It lets a phone or a shop's reader read the watch, and lets the watch read another watch. Two small coils reading each other is the first thing to measure on a bench: it is the main technical risk of this design.

  4. 42 mm across, about 11 mm thick, sealed, with a 20 mm strap on standard bars. Aluminium or steel. This is the part each maker designs for itself.

  5. A flat pad on the side, where a crown would be. The finger is compared inside the sensor's own secure processor: no picture of it is kept and nothing about it leaves the watch. A fingerprint is not a secret, so a finger alone approves only within the day's limit, and the PIN always works in its place.

    Other choice: a sensor under the glass, as on phones. It needs a different kind of screen, which by our estimate empties the battery in about a week.

  6. A small low-power processor that runs the open firmware. It is the only chip wired to the screen, the touch layer and the fingerprint sensor. What you read and what you approve cannot be changed from the radio side.

  7. Two tamper-resistant chips from two different makers: the aim is that a hidden fault in one of them is not enough. The key is made inside a chip and never leaves it. The chips count wrong PIN tries themselves. They sign with Ed25519, the signature Time uses, so the network needs no special rule for a watch.

    Parts that fit today: TROPIC01, whose design is open to audit, and a certified chip such as those of NXP's EdgeLock SE05x family.

  8. NFC for the touch and Bluetooth for the link with a phone: updates, the clock, the balance. No Wi-Fi and no mobile data. The radio chips sit on the other side of one narrow link. They pass signed messages and may ask for a signature. They cannot see a key, draw on the screen or read the glass.

    Parts that fit today: a Bluetooth chip of the class of Nordic's nRF54L15, and an NFC chip that can both read and be read, of the class of ST's ST25R3916B.

  9. A battery of about 200 mAh. A motor that taps the wrist when a payment is sent or received. A sensor in the back that feels the skin, so the watch locks when it leaves the wrist. Two contacts for charging. With no button, the watch restarts when it is put on its charger.

The parts named are examples that meet the need today. No company named on this page is a partner of this project or has reviewed this design.

The rules of the design

  • The key is made inside a secure chip and cannot be read out, by anyone, with any tool the firmware offers.
  • The chips count wrong PIN tries and lock themselves. The count cannot be reset from outside.
  • The fingerprint is compared inside the watch and never stored as a picture.
  • Every payment ends with the finger or the PIN, after the watch has shown who receives and how much. A swipe never sends.
  • The firmware is open, built so that anyone gets the same bytes from the same source, signed by several keys, and never replaced by an older one.
  • Each watch can prove which maker built it and which firmware it runs.
  • An independent laboratory attacks the watch before any maker sells it, and its report is published.

If the watch is stolen

  • It is locked: it left your wrist.
  • The thief needs your finger or your PIN, and the chips allow few tries.
  • At worst the watch can spend its day's limit. The rest of your account needs keys the watch does not hold.
  • From your wallet you remove the watch from your account. Your seconds were never in it.

What it does not protect

  • Someone who forces you to approve.
  • A payment you approve to the wrong person: it is final.
  • A watch from a maker who did not follow the design. Check the maker and the model.
  • Anything at all, today: this is a draft that no laboratory has examined.

Paying, online and offline

  1. One side has a connection first version

    Either side will do. Your watch signs; the side that has a connection carries the payment to the network and both watches show the result. Nobody has to trust the other's watch: the network checks the signature and the balance.

  2. Watch to a phone with no wallet needs a new rule

    Your watch hands the phone a link by touch. The seconds wait on the network for whoever opens the link first, and come back to you if nobody does. The network has no such rule yet.

  3. Both sides offline later, small amounts

    Seconds are set aside on the network and the chip in the watch counts them down. Here only the chip stands in the way of spending twice: a person who breaks open their own chip could pay several people with the same seconds, each up to the limit. So the limit is small, and this comes after the rest.

What works with the network's rules of today: a watch as one more key of an account, under the account's daily limit. A limit of its own for the watch, the link for a phone and paying with both sides offline each need a rule the network does not have yet.

For watch makers

The aim: any maker can build a watch that holds and pays seconds, without asking anyone and without paying anyone.

Three ways to build one

  1. The whole watch

    Built from the reference design: its board, its parts list and its firmware, in a case of your own.

    Security: the full design.

  2. The module in your digital watch

    The secure side as one module: processor, two secure chips, fingerprint sensor. While a payment is on the screen, the module drives your screen and your touch layer and your own software waits.

    Security: the same, checked for each model.

  3. A chip in a classic watch

    A secure chip and a coil under the dial or in the strap, with no battery: the reader powers it, as with a bank card. Hands and movement stay as they are.

    Security: the key is as safe, but the wearer reads the amount on the other device, not on the watch. So it pays small amounts only.

What will be open

  • The touch specification: what two devices say to each other, byte for byte.
  • The firmware of the secure side, under the Apache 2.0 licence, like the rest of Time.
  • The reference board: drawings, layout and parts list, under the CERN Open Hardware Licence, permissive version.
  • The tests a watch must pass, run against the public test network.
  • The screens: what a watch must show before it signs.

Both licences let you build, change and sell without a fee and without permission.

What stays yours

  • The case, the strap, the name and the price.
  • The factory, and the key that marks each watch as yours.
  • The approvals each country asks of a radio product. You are the maker in law. This project sells no watch.

How a watch is recognised

At the factory, your maker's key signs the key of each watch. Makers' keys are kept in an open list beside the source. A wallet can then show who built a watch and which model it is.

An ordinary payment does not rest on this: to the network, a watch is a key with a limit. It matters for paying with both sides offline, where the chip is trusted.

To say a watch follows this design

  1. It passes the public tests on the test network.
  2. Its secure side runs the open firmware, or its changes are published.
  3. An independent laboratory has attacked it, and the report is public.
  4. Its maker's key is in the open list.

Where this stands

Draft of 27 November 2026.

Exists

  • This design draft and the demonstration on this page.
  • The Time test network and its open source, where a watch would be one more key of an account.

Does not exist yet

  • Any watch, board or prototype.
  • The firmware and the touch specification.
  • A measurement of two small antennas reading each other, or of the battery.
  • A laboratory's examination.
  • The network rules named under Paying.

The order of work

  1. The touch specification, reviewed in the open.
  2. A bench prototype on development boards: the touch, finger to chip to a signed payment on the test network, the battery draw.
  3. A few watches that can be worn, with the real chips.
  4. The laboratory, then the first maker.

Still open

  • How the two secure chips share the work, so that breaking one is not enough.
  • The sensor on the side or under the glass.
  • How two watches pay each other when both phones are out of reach.
